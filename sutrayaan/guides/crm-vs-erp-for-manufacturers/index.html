CRM or ERP: which does a manufacturer need first?

By Sutrayaan · Updated 14 November 2026 · 7-minute read

Short answer: a CRM manages everything before the order: enquiries, quotes and follow-ups. An ERP manages everything after the order: materials, production, stock, dispatch and accounts. If you are losing enquiries or quotes go unanswered, start with a CRM. It costs less and is live in weeks. If orders are won but production, stock or billing are chaos, you need an ERP. Most small manufacturers already run Tally for accounts and should add a CRM first.

One order, two systems

Follow a single order through your factory. The CRM covers the first half; the ERP covers the second.

  1. EnquiryWebsite, IndiaMART, call
  2. QuotePrice, drawing, approval
  3. Follow-upUntil won or lost
  4. OrderSales order, specs
  5. MaterialsPurchase, stock
  6. ProductionBOM, job cards
  7. Dispatch and invoiceE-way bill, GST, payment
CRM · before the order · "Did we win it?"
ERP · after the order · "Can we make, ship and bill it?"

The handover point is the won deal. A good setup sends a won deal from the CRM to the ERP or accounts as a sales order, so nobody types the details twice.

Why most small manufacturers should start with a CRM

  • You probably have part of an ERP already. Tally handles accounts, GST, inventory and basic job work for most small factories. One small-business guide suggests TallyPrime for most small Indian manufacturers, moving to an ERP once you need work orders and production planning.6
  • Nothing handles the sales side. Enquiries sit in inboxes, WhatsApp and notebooks. That is where orders are lost, and no accounting software fixes it.
  • It is cheaper and faster. A small team can be on a CRM within a week. An ERP for a factory takes months.
  • It is lower risk. If a CRM rollout stumbles, sales carries on. If an ERP rollout stumbles, billing and GST filings can stop.

When you do need an ERP

  • You plan production from orders, with a bill of materials for each product
  • You need to know the real cost of each job or part
  • You run more than one plant, warehouse or GST number
  • Stock, purchase and production teams work from different spreadsheets
  • Your turnover and team have outgrown Tally plus Excel. One Indian comparison puts that line around ₹15 crore turnover.5

How the two fit together

  1. Start with a CRM and keep Tally for accounts.
  2. Connect them at the won deal. Won deals go to accounts as a clean sales order instead of being re-typed.
  3. Add inventory or an ERP once stock and production become the bottleneck.
  4. Keep one customer list. Whatever you choose, the customer name, GST number and address should come from one place.

Four mistakes to avoid

  1. Buying an ERP to fix a sales problem. ERPs start at the order. They don't chase enquiries.
  2. Automating a process nobody has agreed. Write down who handles an enquiry and when a quote goes out first. Software follows the process, not the other way round.
  3. Replacing Tally because a vendor says so. If accounts are happy, connect to Tally instead.
  4. Doing everything at once. One system at a time, each fully in use before the next.

Is Tally an ERP?

Tally is accounting software with GST, inventory and some manufacturing features. For many small factories it does part of an ERP's job. It does not manage enquiries, quotes or follow-ups, which is what a CRM is for.

Can one system do both CRM and ERP?

Some suites cover both, such as Zoho's apps, Odoo or SAP. Even then, most firms roll out the sales side and the operations side separately, one after the other.

Will a CRM work with Tally?

Yes. The usual approach is to keep Tally for accounts and send won deals or customer details from the CRM to Tally, using a connector or a simple export. Check what your CRM plan supports.

How long does each take to set up?

A simple CRM: one to eight weeks. Tally add-ons: two to four weeks. A full ERP with a partner: two to twelve months, depending on the product and scope.

We are a 15-person job shop. What do we need?

Usually Tally for accounts and a simple CRM such as Bigin for enquiries and quotes. Revisit an ERP when stock or production planning becomes the main problem.
